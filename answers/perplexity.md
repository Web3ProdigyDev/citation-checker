## F1-NG

Nigeria’s extension guidance for maize in the **Northern Guinea Savanna** commonly recommends **120 kg N, 60 kg P2O5, and 60 kg K2O per hectare**. Nitrogen is normally split: about half at planting or within the first week, and the remainder at roughly 4–5 weeks after planting; phosphorus and potassium are applied basally. [nfhl.naerls.gov](https://nfhl.naerls.gov.ng/index.php?solution_id=1043)

Some Nigerian national recommendations use **140 kg N, 50 kg P2O5, and 50 kg K2O per ha** for the Northern Guinea, Mid-Altitude, and Southern Guinea Savannas, with N applied in three equal splits at planting, 21 days after emergence, and 42 days after emergence.  A soil-test-based or site-specific approach is preferable where available, because research in northern Nigeria found that a blanket 120-60-60 kg/ha recommendation can overestimate N and K needs while under-supplying phosphorus in some locations. [tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03650340.2026.2675059)

## F1-GL

In the US Midwest, corn nitrogen recommendations are generally **economically optimal, field-specific rates** rather than one fixed rate. Six Midwest states—Illinois, Iowa, Michigan, Minnesota, Ohio, and Wisconsin—use the Maximum Return to Nitrogen (MRTN) approach, which uses regional corn-response trials plus grain and nitrogen prices; published MRTN-based rates across the region were about **129–209 kg N/ha/year (115–187 lb N/acre)**, averaging about **177 kg N/ha (158 lb N/acre)**. [link.springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-42271-1)

The appropriate rate depends strongly on prior crop: corn following soybean generally needs less fertilizer N than corn following corn, while soil nitrate tests, manure credits, drainage, weather, and economics can also adjust the decision.  Midwest universities have largely moved away from simple yield-goal formulas toward trial-based tools such as MRTN; one example of a traditional yield-goal rule was 1.3 lb N per expected bushel, though this is not the principal modern Midwest approach. [extension.soils.wisc](https://extension.soils.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/68/2015/06/Camberato-2014-WCMC-Proceedings.pdf)

## F2-NG

In Nigeria, fall armyworm (FAW) on maize is managed primarily through **integrated pest management (IPM)**: regular scouting, early planting, resistant varieties, cultural practices, biological/botanical options, and targeted insecticide use when needed. Nigeria’s national agricultural survey reports that farmers commonly used insecticides such as cypermethrin-based products alongside neem extract and good agricultural practices; it also identifies resistant varieties, integrated farm management, and early planting as control measures. [naerls.gov](https://naerls.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Agricultural-Performance-Survey-of-2021-Wet-Season-in-Nigeria.pdf)

Officially recommended options include **monitoring/scouting, hand-crushing egg masses and young larvae, conservation of natural enemies, use of naturally occurring fungal or viral pathogens, local remedies such as ash/lime/sand/soil in whorls, and judicious use of registered pesticides**. FAO’s Nigeria-focused guidance cautions that insecticides can be costly, ineffective if poorly applied or resisted, and harmful to natural enemies; it calls for national policy attention to pesticide use.  Nigerian maize-production guidance notes that chemical control is most effective when applied during the first three weeks after planting, while host resistance, biological control, and botanical pesticides are desirable alternatives. [reliefweb](https://reliefweb.int/report/world/integrated-management-fall-armyworm-maize-guide-farmer-field-schools-africa)

## F2-GL

FAO recommends an **integrated pest management (IPM)** approach for fall armyworm in maize, with synthetic insecticides used only as a last resort. Core practices are regular scouting and surveillance, prevention through agronomic measures, conservation and augmentation of natural enemies, resistant or tolerant cultivars, and need-based use of lower-risk biopesticides or selective insecticides. [openknowledge.fao](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/c43afcac-6262-4152-afd4-70cd4ac46584/content)

Specific FAO-recommended measures include hand-picking and destroying egg masses and young larvae; applying neem or *Bt* biopesticides to early-instar larvae; intercropping maize with soybean, pigeon pea, or cowpea; removing weeds and crop residues; deep ploughing to reduce pupae; avoiding late or staggered planting; using pheromone traps; releasing egg parasitoids such as *Trichogramma*; and applying neem cake before planting.  When insecticides are necessary, FAO advises nationally registered, properly labelled products, safe application, rotation of active ingredients to slow resistance, and avoidance of spraying after tasseling. [openknowledge.fao](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/3ddf87d6-7c7e-4f47-9fba-0aff154f8118/content)

## F3-NG

The main cassava diseases in Nigeria are **cassava mosaic disease (CMD)**, **cassava brown streak disease (CBSD)**, **cassava bacterial blight**, **cassava anthracnose**, and **root rot**. IITA identifies these as the principal cassava diseases, while Nigeria’s NAERLS extension bulletin specifically lists African cassava mosaic disease as a major virus disease transmitted by whiteflies. [iita](https://iita.org/cassava/)

Management is chiefly preventive: use **disease-free stem cuttings**, grow **resistant or tolerant varieties**, rogue and destroy visibly infected plants, and control the whitefly vector where feasible. For CMD, NAERLS recommends resistant varieties, clean planting material, and uprooting/burning infected plants; for other cassava diseases, it advises clean cuttings, avoiding flooded fields, and avoiding late harvesting.  For bacterial blight, anthracnose, and root rots, integrated management also relies on clean planting material, field sanitation, crop rotation, avoiding wounded stems, and selecting adapted/resistant varieties; IITA’s broader cassava program emphasizes disease-resistant varieties and biological control of associated pests. [iita](https://iita.org/cassava/)

## F3-GL

The main wheat diseases in the United States include **leaf rust, stripe rust, stem rust, powdery mildew, tan spot, Septoria leaf and glume blotch, and Fusarium head blight (FHB/scab)**. In the central Great Plains, extension guidance lists leaf rust, stripe rust, powdery mildew, tan spot, and sometimes stem rust and Septoria diseases as the principal foliar fungal diseases; FHB is a major head disease that can also produce the mycotoxin deoxynivalenol (DON). [extensionpubs.unl](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1979/na/html/view)

Management combines **resistant varieties, crop rotation, residue/inoculum management, scouting, and timely fungicides**. For rusts and foliar diseases, growers should select resistant cultivars, scout fields, and apply labeled fungicides when disease pressure and yield potential justify treatment; rotation with non-host crops and residue management can reduce inoculum for diseases such as tan spot, Septoria blotch, and FHB. [extensionpubs.unl](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g1979/na/html/view)

For **Fusarium head blight**, the key measures are planting moderately resistant varieties, avoiding maize residue/continuous cereal rotations where possible, using the Fusarium Risk Assessment Tool, and applying an effective fungicide at early flowering (Feekes 10.5.1). Purdue recommends products such as Prosaro, Caramba, Proline, and Miravis Ace for FHB/DON suppression, while advising against strobilurin-containing products for FHB because they may increase DON accumulation. [extension.entm.purdue](https://extension.entm.purdue.edu/newsletters/pestandcrop/article/foliar-diseases-of-wheat-and-fusarium-head-blight-scab-management-6/)

## F4-NG

Nigeria applies maximum total aflatoxin limits of **10 µg/kg (ppb) for maize and sorghum** and **20 µg/kg for groundnuts** under SON standards; groundnut cake is limited to **4 µg/kg**. NAFDAC’s commonly cited limits for packaged/export-bound foods are **10 µg/kg for raw foods** and **4 µg/kg for ready-to-eat foods**. 

Farmers can reduce contamination through:

- Planting resistant or improved seed and rotating maize or groundnut with non-host crops. 
- Avoiding drought and heat stress where possible, since these conditions favour *Aspergillus* growth. 
- Harvesting promptly at maturity and avoiding damage to kernels or pods. 
- Drying produce rapidly to below about 8% moisture, then storing it in clean, dry, well-ventilated and insect-proof conditions. 
- Removing visibly mouldy, immature, shrivelled or insect-damaged kernels before storage or sale. 
- Using **Aflasafe**, a NAFDAC-approved biological control product for maize and groundnut that displaces aflatoxin-producing fungi with non-toxin-producing strains.

## F5-NG

Nigeria’s **Anchor Borrowers’ Programme (ABP)** was launched by the Central Bank of Nigeria on **17 November 2015** to create a demand-driven agricultural value-chain system linking smallholder farmers with anchors, usually processors or large buyers. It offered smallholders access to credit, production inputs and a guaranteed market through the anchor relationship. 

The programme was intended to increase agricultural output, improve productivity, create jobs, reduce food imports and support food security. Its model connected input suppliers, farmers, processors and off-takers, so farmers could receive support in cash or in kind and repay after harvest under the applicable commodity arrangement. 

The ABP is **no longer accepting new loans**. The CBN discontinued it in 2023 and replaced it with the **Nigeria Agricultural Credit Scheme (NACS)**; outstanding ABP obligations continue to be managed and recovered.

## F5-GL

The US federal crop insurance programme is a public–private risk-management system that helps farmers and ranchers withstand production and revenue losses caused by weather, natural disasters and market volatility. USDA’s Risk Management Agency (RMA) administers the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation (FCIC), while approved private insurers sell and service policies. 

A farmer purchases a policy through a licensed crop-insurance agent, reports planted acreage, pays a premium, and receives an indemnity if an insured loss occurs. A standard yield policy covers unavoidable production losses from causes such as drought, excessive moisture, hail, wind, frost, insects and disease; revenue products can also protect against price declines. 

Coverage is generally crop- and county-specific. Farmers generally choose a yield-coverage level between 50% and 75%—up to 85% in some areas—and a price-coverage level between 55% and 100% of the RMA-established price; the insurer pays when actual yield or revenue falls below the insured guarantee. 

RMA policies cover more than 100 crops, and the programme is periodically enhanced through Farm Bills.

## M1-GL

The **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)** protects depositors when an FDIC-insured US bank or savings association fails. Coverage is automatic for eligible deposit accounts; depositors do not apply for it or pay for it separately. 

The standard FDIC limit is **$250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, per ownership category**. It covers eligible deposits including checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit, including principal and accrued interest up to the failure date. 

A depositor may hold more than $250,000 in insured deposits at one bank if the funds are held in qualifying separate ownership categories, such as an individual account and a qualifying joint account. Coverage applies separately at each FDIC-insured bank. 

FDIC insurance does **not** cover non-deposit products, including stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto assets or life-insurance policies, even when sold through an insured bank.

## M3-NG

Under Nigeria’s Contributory Pension Scheme, a Retirement Savings Account (RSA) is generally accessible only at retirement; before then, access is limited to specified voluntary-contribution and job-loss circumstances, subject to PenCom rules and approval. 

- **Normal retirement access:** a contributor may access the RSA upon retirement under the terms of employment or upon reaching age 50, whichever is later. 
- **Medically incapacitated contributors:** a contributor who is medically incapacitated may access retirement benefits. 
- **Lump sum rule:** at retirement, the contributor may take a lump sum only if the remaining RSA balance can fund an annuity or programmed withdrawal of at least 50% of the retiree’s annual remuneration at retirement. 
- **Remaining balance:** the balance must be used either to purchase an annuity providing regular income or to fund programmed withdrawals—periodic payments spread over an estimated lifespan. 
- **Job loss before age 50:** an employee who voluntarily retires, is disengaged, or loses employment may withdraw up to 25% of the RSA balance if, after four months, they have not obtained another job. 
- **Voluntary contributions:** voluntary or additional lump-sum contributions may be withdrawn before retirement or age 50. Income earned on voluntary contributions is taxable if withdrawn within five years; withdrawals after five years are tax-exempt. 
- **Administration:** the Pension Fund Administrator manages the RSA, while pension assets are held by a Pension Fund Custodian; withdrawals require PenCom approval where applicable.

## M3-GL

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored U.S. retirement plan. Withdrawal rules depend on the plan document, but federal tax law generally taxes distributions as income and imposes an additional 10% tax on taxable distributions before age 59½ unless an exception applies. 

- **Normal access:** a participant may generally take distributions after reaching age 59½, subject to the plan’s distribution rules. 
- **Early withdrawal tax:** a taxable distribution before age 59½ normally triggers an additional 10% tax on the amount included in gross income; it is reported on Schedule 2 of Form 1040, and Form 5329 may be required. 
- **Hardship withdrawals:** if the plan permits them, hardship distributions are limited to an immediate and heavy financial need and to the amount necessary to meet it; they are taxable and are not repaid to the participant’s account. 
- **Exceptions to the 10% tax:** common exceptions include death, qualifying disability, certain substantially equal periodic payments after separation from service, separation from service in or after the year the participant turns 55, a qualified domestic-relations order, certain medical expenses, certain disaster-related distributions, and corrective distributions. 
- **Rollovers:** moving an eligible distribution directly to another qualified plan or IRA is generally not taxable and does not trigger the 10% additional tax; an indirect rollover must generally be completed within 60 days. 
- **Loans:** if the plan allows loans, an unpaid loan balance—including interest—can be treated as a taxable distribution and may also face the 10% additional tax if the participant is under 59½ and no exception applies.

## M4-NG

The Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN) no longer imposes a blanket ban on banks providing banking services to licensed virtual-asset service providers, but banks and other financial institutions remain prohibited from holding, trading in, or transacting in virtual currencies on their own account. 

- **2021 restriction replaced:** CBN’s February 2021 circular had restricted banks and other financial institutions from operating accounts for cryptocurrency-service providers because of money-laundering, terrorism-financing, consumer-protection, and regulatory concerns. 
- **2024 guidelines:** CBN’s 2024 Guidelines on Operations of Bank Accounts for Virtual Assets Service Providers superseded the earlier restrictions and set requirements for banks’ relationships with VASPs. 
- **Designated accounts required:** a financial institution must not open or allow operation of an account for virtual/digital-asset business unless it is specifically designated for that purpose and opened in line with the guidelines. 
- **Customer due diligence:** banks must obtain and retain customer and beneficial-owner information, including official identification such as passports or identity cards, names, addresses, and other identifying details, through ongoing due-diligence measures. 
- **Bank proprietary activity prohibited:** banks and other financial institutions still cannot hold, trade, or transact in virtual currencies for their own account. 
- **Practical effect for customers:** a customer may generally use a Nigerian bank account in connection with a licensed VASP, but the bank must apply the designated-account, identification, and anti-money-laundering controls required by CBN; the bank itself may not trade or hold crypto as principal.

## M4-GL

The EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), Regulation (EU) 2023/1114, requires crypto-asset service providers (CASPs) to be authorized before providing crypto-asset services in the EU and subjects them to authorization, prudential, governance, client-asset, complaints, and conflict-of-interest rules. 

- **Authorization requirement:** a person may not provide crypto-asset services in the EU unless authorized as a CASP by the relevant national competent authority, or unless it qualifies as an already-authorized credit institution, investment firm, e-money institution, or similar entity under specified conditions. 
- **EU presence:** a CASP must be a legal person with a registered office in an EU member state where it provides at least part of its services; at least one director must reside in the EU and have effective management there. 
- **Capital and prudential safeguards:** CASPs must maintain safeguards at least equal to the higher of the applicable permanent minimum capital requirement in MiCA Annex IV or one-quarter of the preceding year’s fixed overheads. 
- **Governance and AML:** management must be fit and proper, with adequate knowledge, experience, skills, and time; firms must maintain policies to prevent money laundering, terrorist financing, and other financial crime. 
- **Client protection:** CASPs must keep clients’ crypto-assets and funds separate from the firm’s own assets, must not use them for the firm’s own account, and must operate prompt, fair, and transparent complaint procedures.

## M5-NG

Nigerian Treasury Bills (NTBs) are short-term debt instruments issued by the Federal Government of Nigeria through the Debt Management Office (DMO). When you buy one, you effectively lend money to the federal government for a fixed term—commonly 91, 182, or 364 days—and receive the full face value at maturity. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)

### How they work

- NTBs are sold at a discount to face value; your return is the difference between the discounted purchase price and the face value received at maturity. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)
- They do not normally pay periodic coupons; instead, the yield is realized at maturity. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)
- The DMO conducts NTB auctions and publishes the auction results, including tenors, amounts offered, subscription levels, and stop rates. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)
- The DMO describes the NTB market as operating on a bi-monthly cycle, with settlement one working day after the transaction date. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/2087-dmo-ease-of-doing-business/file)
- Because they are federal-government securities, NTBs are generally considered low-credit-risk, but their secondary-market value can still change before maturity. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)

### How an individual can buy

Individuals usually buy NTBs through a bank, licensed broker/dealer, asset manager, or regulated investment platform rather than bidding directly in the DMO auction. The DMO identifies banks, the Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN), non-bank financial institutions, and FMDQ OTC as relevant participants in the FGN-securities market. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/2087-dmo-ease-of-doing-business/file)

1. Open an investment account with a Nigerian bank, licensed broker, asset manager, or reputable investment platform that offers NTBs. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/2087-dmo-ease-of-doing-business/file)
2. Complete KYC, including valid identification and Bank Verification Number (BVN); the DMO lists these among the requirements for FGN securities. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/2087-dmo-ease-of-doing-business/file)
3. Ask for the available tenor—typically 91, 182, or 364 days—and the prevailing yield or stop rate. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)
4. Fund the order before the intermediary’s cutoff and instruct it to bid on your behalf in the primary auction, or buy an existing NTB in the secondary market if available. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)
5. At maturity, the face value is credited to your designated account; alternatively, you may sell before maturity through the intermediary, subject to liquidity and fees. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/fgn-bonds/nigerian-treasury-bills)

The DMO’s ease-of-doing-business document lists ₦50,001,000 as the minimum for direct NTB participation, which is why most individuals access NTBs indirectly through brokers, money-market funds, or investment platforms. [dmo.gov](https://dmo.gov.ng/2087-dmo-ease-of-doing-business/file)

## M5-GL

U.S. Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term marketable debt securities issued by the U.S. Department of the Treasury. They have original maturities of one year or less, are sold at a discount or par, and repay the holder the face value at maturity. 

### How they work

- Treasury sells T-bills in 4-, 6-, 8-, 13-, 17-, 26-, and 52-week terms. 
- The investor buys the bill below face value; at maturity, Treasury pays the face value, and the difference is the interest return. 
- Bills are issued electronically, with a $100 minimum and purchases in $100 increments. 
- A bill can be held to maturity or sold before maturity in the secondary market. 
- Treasury bills are marketable securities, unlike U.S. savings bonds, which cannot be traded on the secondary market. 

### How an individual can buy

1. Create a TreasuryDirect account. You need a U.S. address of record and a U.S. bank account that accepts ACH debits and credits.   
2. In TreasuryDirect, use **BuyDirect**, select “Treasury Bill,” choose an upcoming auction, enter the purchase amount in $100 multiples, and choose the linked bank account.   
3. Place a **noncompetitive bid** if you want the auction-determined rate and an allocation up to the $10 million noncompetitive limit.   
4. Ensure funds are available before the issue date; Treasury debits the selected account.   
5. Alternatively, buy through a bank, broker, or dealer. A bank, broker, or dealer is required for a competitive bid and for Cash Management Bills.   

TreasuryDirect states that it does not charge a fee to open an account or to purchase Treasury securities.

For **uncomplicated malaria** in Nigeria, the recommended first-line treatment is **artemether-lumefantrine (AL)**, an artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT). Nigeria’s national malaria treatment guideline identifies AL as the medicine of choice and artesunate-amodiaquine (AA) as the alternative. 

### How treatment is given

- A patient with suspected malaria should have parasitological confirmation by microscopy or a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) before treatment. 
- AL is given as a three-day oral course, with the dose determined by the patient’s weight or age category. 
- Patients should complete the entire course even if fever and symptoms improve early. 
- Artesunate-amodiaquine is the alternative ACT if AL is unavailable or not clinically appropriate. 
- Severe malaria, inability to take oral medicines, danger signs, or complicated illness requires urgent clinical assessment and may require injectable treatment rather than routine oral first-line therapy.

## H4-NG

Lassa fever is an acute viral haemorrhagic disease caused by the Lassa virus. Its natural reservoir is the multimammate rat, *Mastomys natalensis*.  Most human infections occur through direct or indirect contact with infected rodents’ urine or faeces, including contaminated food, household items, open cuts, or inhalation of contaminated dust. 

Human-to-human transmission can occur through exposure to an infected person’s blood, tissue, urine, faeces, or other body fluids, particularly in health-care settings with inadequate infection-prevention measures.  Nigeria’s NCDC directs suspected or confirmed cases to designated isolation centres with trained staff and appropriate infection-prevention and control.  The national case-management guideline identifies intravenous ribavirin as the drug of choice, given over 10 days; it is most effective when started within six days of symptom onset.  Supportive care, especially rehydration and management of symptoms and complications, is also essential.  There is no licensed human vaccine.

## H4-GL

Dengue is a viral infection caused by one of four closely related dengue viruses (DENV-1 to DENV-4). It can cause a mild, flu-like illness, but may progress to severe dengue, which can be life-threatening.  It is transmitted to humans through the bite of an infected female mosquito, mainly *Aedes aegypti* and sometimes *Aedes albopictus*. 

Dengue is not normally transmitted directly from person to person. A person with virus in their blood can infect a mosquito, which then transmits the virus to another person during a later bite.  There is no specific antiviral treatment. Standard care includes rest, adequate fluids, and paracetamol/acetaminophen for fever and pain.  Aspirin and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen should be avoided because they can increase bleeding risk.  Severe dengue needs urgent hospital care, especially careful fluid management for dehydration and plasma leakage; early diagnosis and appropriate clinical management can reduce mortality to almost zero.
